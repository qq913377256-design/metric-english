## English Original

### Make a Decision Under Uncertainty

Fictional platform RiverDesk had evidence that a new fraud rule would reduce losses, but the estimate ranged from 8% to 24%. The rule might also block good customers. Waiting three months would provide better labels, while fraud losses would continue. Leaders asked for a yes-or-no answer that the data could not honestly provide.

The analyst framed the decision around expected value, downside, reversibility, and cost of delay. A full launch had high upside and high customer risk. Doing nothing avoided false blocks but preserved losses. A 10% rollout with manual review limited downside and generated evidence. Stop conditions included a false-block rate above 0.6% or no measurable loss reduction after four weeks.

She also estimated the value of additional information. Better labels could materially change the choice, but three months of waiting cost more than a controlled pilot. The pilot collected the specific outcomes needed to narrow uncertainty. Customer support and risk teams owned guardrails and rollback.

The recommendation stated assumptions and ranges rather than hiding them in an average. It separated uncertainty that could be learned from uncertainty the business simply had to accept. Weekly checkpoints allowed expansion, modification, or stop. Because the action was reversible, the evidence threshold could be lower than for an irreversible policy.

RiverDesk launched the pilot and expanded only after guardrails held. Decision quality was judged by whether the process used available evidence and controlled risk, not by pretending the eventual outcome had been known. Under uncertainty, the goal is rarely perfect prediction. It is a decision whose size, speed, and safeguards fit what is known.

The team documented why a pilot was preferable to waiting, which losses were acceptable, and who could stop expansion. If later results disappointed, reviewers could judge the original decision against information available at the time. This reduced hindsight bias and encouraged honest uncertainty.

## Vocabulary
- **downside** — 下行风险；potential harmful outcome.
- **cost of delay** — 等待成本；value lost while postponing action.
- **false block** — 误拦截；a legitimate customer incorrectly stopped.
- **value of information** — 信息价值；benefit of learning before deciding.
- **reversibility** — 可逆性；how easily a decision can be undone.
- **stop condition** — 停止条件；a predefined signal that ends a rollout.
- **rollback** — 回滚；returning safely to the previous rule or version.
- **hindsight bias** — 事后偏见；judging a past decision using information learned later.
## Grammar Analysis
- “uncertainty **that could be learned from**”用被动结构限定可缩小的不确定性。
- “whose size, speed, and safeguards fit...”用whose说明决策属性。

## Reading Questions
1. Why was 10% rollout preferred?
2. What was the value of labels?
3. How should decision quality be judged?
### Workplace Application
Evidence is promising but uncertain and the action is reversible. Write a 40–80 word staged decision with guardrails and a learning checkpoint.
### Answer Key
1. A 10% rollout limited downside while generating evidence.
2. Better labels could materially change the decision, but waiting carried a higher cost.
3. Decision quality should be judged using the evidence available at the time and the controls applied.

### Application Model Response

I recommend a 10% pilot with manual review because the expected benefit is promising and the action is reversible. Risk and Support will monitor false blocks and losses weekly. We will stop if false blocks exceed 0.6%, and expand after four weeks only if losses fall meaningfully while customer guardrails remain within range.
