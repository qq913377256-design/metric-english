## English Original

### Correlation Does Not Explain the Cause

Fictional fitness platform PulsePath found that users who joined social groups exercised 40% more often than users who did not. A presentation claimed that groups caused higher activity and recommended forcing every new user into one. The data showed a relationship, but not the reason for it. Highly motivated users might both join groups and exercise more.

Motivation was a confounder: a factor related to both the proposed cause and the outcome. Other differences also mattered. Group members had used the app longer, owned more connected devices, and were more likely to have paid plans. Comparing raw averages mixed the possible group effect with these pre-existing characteristics. Reverse causality was possible too: frequent exercisers may have discovered groups because they used the app more.

The analyst revised the language from “groups increase exercise” to “group membership is associated with higher exercise frequency.” She adjusted the comparison for tenure, plan, prior activity, and device ownership, but explained that adjustment covered only measured factors. The estimated difference became smaller and still could not prove causality.

To support a causal decision, PulsePath designed an experiment. Eligible new users were randomly offered a guided group experience or the standard onboarding. The team measured group participation, exercise frequency, retention, and notification opt-outs. Random assignment made baseline differences less likely to explain the outcome. For users who could not be randomized, the team considered natural experiments and stronger observational designs, while stating their assumptions.

Correlation is valuable. It can identify patterns, improve prediction, and suggest hypotheses. The error occurs when analysts move from pattern to mechanism without sufficient design or evidence. Useful reports separate what was observed, what may explain it, what alternative explanations remain, and what test could reduce uncertainty. PulsePath did not abandon the social feature. It changed an unsupported causal claim into a testable business question.

## Chinese Translation

PulsePath发现加入社群的用户运动频率高40%，但高动机用户可能既更愿意入群，也更常运动；使用时长、付费和设备也不同，还可能是高频用户先运动、后发现社群。

分析师把“导致”改为“相关”，控制已测因素但说明仍不能证明因果。随后随机提供引导社群体验，通过实验减少基线差异。相关性可发现模式与假设，不能在缺少设计时直接解释机制。

## Vocabulary

- **confounder** — 混杂因素；a factor related to both exposure and outcome.
- **reverse causality** — 反向因果；the outcome may influence the proposed cause.
- **random assignment** — 随机分配；allocation by chance.
- **observational design** — 观察性设计；analysis without random assignment.
- **hypothesis** — 假设；a testable proposed explanation.

## Grammar Analysis

- “users **who joined social groups**”用who限定比较人群。
- “made baseline differences less likely **to explain**”使用不定式说明更不可能发生的作用。

## Data Analyst Extension

结论模板分四段：observed pattern、possible mechanism、alternative explanations、next test。没有随机或可信准实验设计时，优先使用associated with而不是caused by。

## Reading Questions

1. What was the main confounder?
2. Why was adjustment insufficient?
3. How did random assignment help?

### Workplace Application

Premium users retain twice as long as free users. Write a 40–80 word note that avoids a causal claim and proposes evidence needed before recommending premium incentives.

### Answer Key

1. Motivation was the main confounder.
2. Adjustment could not remove unmeasured differences.
3. Random assignment reduced systematic baseline differences.

### Application Model Response

Premium membership is associated with longer retention, but this comparison does not show that premium causes the difference. Users who choose premium may already have higher intent, income, or prior engagement. Before offering incentives, I recommend a randomized trial or a credible quasi-experiment that measures retention, cost, and customer guardrails for otherwise comparable users.
